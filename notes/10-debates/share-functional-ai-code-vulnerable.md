## Question

The question is what share of the code that AI generators produce, and especially of the code that works, is vulnerable. It is contested in benchmark evaluations of a code assistant in 2021 and of LLMs and coding agents in 2024–2026. Each source defines "vulnerable" in its own way and divides by its own denominator. One counts valid completions, one counts functionally correct backends, and one counts functionally correct repository patches. The figures are therefore not one quantity measured three times. What is contested is how large the vulnerable share is under each source's measure, and whether it is the majority of output.

## Positions

### Most correct output is insecure

The SUSVIBES paper (Zhao et al.) holds that most functionally correct agent output is insecure. Its benchmark has 186 repository-level feature-request tasks from 100 Python repositories, covering 79 CWE types (p. 5). Security tests come from the human-written tests added by real vulnerability-fix commits, and a solution's security is counted only when it is also functionally correct (p. 6). SWE-Agent with Claude 4 Sonnet solves 57.0% of tasks functionally but only 11.8% also securely. The paper reports from this that **79.3%** of its functionally correct solutions are insecure (p. 6, p. 7). The figure for OpenHands with Claude 4 Sonnet is 77.8% (p. 6). Average SecPass across the 12 agent-model settings is around 10% (p. 6). The authors put it as: "around 80% of the time" a vibe-coding user who accepts a solution after it passes functional tests leaves security vulnerabilities in the repository (p. 6). They state that test outcomes are a proxy for security and that CWE annotations and tests may be insufficient (p. 24). They also report that 6.5% of the security tests are coupled to the reference implementation, so alternative secure implementations can fail them (p. 4, p. 19).

BaxBench (Vero et al.) qualifies the position. It runs 11 LLMs on 392 backend-generation tasks and executes expert-written exploits against the running applications (p. 3, p. 4, p. 5). It reports that, on average, exploits succeed against **around half** of the correct programs generated by each model (p. 1, p. 5). The best correct-and-secure rate is about 37%, against a best pass@1 of 62% (p. 2, p. 5). On less popular or multi-file frameworks such as Rust-Actix and PHP-Lumen, the paper finds that correct solutions are more often vulnerable (p. 6). It treats its measured sec_pass as a strict upper bound on true security, since exploits may miss vulnerabilities (p. 24).

The Broken by Default study (Blain and Noiseux) reports a majority figure over all generated artifacts. Across seven production LLMs and 500 security-sensitive prompts in five CWE categories, **55.8%** of 3,500 artifacts contained at least one vulnerability identified by the authors' COBALT engine, with 1,055 findings proven by Z3 satisfiability witnesses (p. 1, p. 3). Per-model rates run from 48.4% (Gemini 2.5 Flash) to 62.4% (GPT-4o) (p. 3), and category rates from 25% for cryptography to 87% for integer arithmetic (p. 4). The denominator is all artifacts, generated at temperature 0 (p. 2, p. 3). The headline rate includes pattern-match findings without Z3 confirmation, and the paper reports no false-positive rate for COBALT (p. 3, p. 4). The authors are COBALT's developers (p. 1).

The metamorphic-testing study by Peng, Wang and Zhu reports that 2,545 of 3,700 snippets (**68.8%**) violate at least one of nine security-oriented metamorphic relations (p. 3). The snippets come from five open models of 4.5B to 30B on 148 LLMSecEval prompts, five runs each, and the denominator is all generated snippets (p. 3). Violations are judged by an LLM (Claude Sonnet 4.6) that reasons about the code statically without executing it (p. 2), and the paper gives no measured precision or recall for the judge (p. 5). Model rates range from 65.1% to 73.8% (p. 3).

The poster by Ma et al. reports baseline vulnerability rates above 56% for all eight LLMs it evaluates, on 2,700 prompt pairs in nine languages built around three prompt-side risk scenarios (p. 3, p. 4, p. 5). The rate is 66% under ambiguous requirements and 52% under a security–functionality conflict (p. 5). The denominator is the sampled outputs, each labelled secure or vulnerable by static analysis (Clang Static Analyzer, CodeQL) and expert review assisted by GPT-5, with the final label set by expert review (p. 4). The prompts are designed to resemble vague, incomplete or security-conflicting developer requests, rather than prompts that state security requirements (p. 1).

Kahn and Hastings report that every one of nine Python automation scripts, from ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot and Google Gemini on three tasks, contained exploitable vulnerabilities, and that all nine executed and met their functional requirements (p. 7). The denominator is these nine functional scripts, one generation per model and task (p. 3). The findings come from a single Claude Code review capped at five findings per file, with no manual confirmation and no exploitation, and the authors state that the cap may have hidden further vulnerabilities (p. 3, p. 8).

### A substantial minority is insecure

The Copilot study by Pearce et al. reports that approximately **40%** of the programs it generated were vulnerable, across 89 scenarios and 1,689 programs (p. 1). By experiment, 44.00% of 1,084 valid programs in the weakness-diversity set contained the targeted CWE (p. 5). Overall, 39.33% of top-ranked options and 40.73% of all options were vulnerable (p. 13). The denominator is valid programs, meaning syntactically compliant, compilable and unique (p. 5). Functional correctness is not assessed (p. 4, p. 5). An option counts as vulnerable only if it definitively contains the one targeted weakness, which the authors describe as a conservative classification (p. 5). The share varies widely by weakness and language. It was 60% of options for CWE-22 against 19% for CWE-79 (p. 9), and 50.29% in C against 38.35% in Python (p. 5). The authors acknowledge that CodeQL may have marked some safe code vulnerable, and that sample sizes may not support statistical conclusions (p. 13).

Liu et al. give ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) the 54 CWE scenarios of Pearce et al. and report that 994 of 2,983 valid snippets (**33.32%**) were vulnerable, 51.64% in C against 17.08% in Python3 (p. 22, p. 26). Detection is by CodeQL, with manual checking by the authors for some scenarios (p. 22). The denominator is valid snippets (p. 26). The authors note that CodeQL may report secure code as vulnerable (p. 32). The text gives 60 generations per scenario, while the valid counts in its table imply smaller denominators in several rows (p. 22, p. 23).

Datta, Aljohani and Do report that roughly 25–33% of the programs generated by five open code models on Instruct Prime (1,404 prompts, eight languages, 50 CWE types) are insecure at a zero-shot baseline (p. 1, p. 3, p. 6). Per-model secure rates range from 68.30% (OpenCoder) to 72.86% (QwenCoder) (p. 6). Insecurity is flagged by the Insecure Code Detector (ICD), a static CWE-aligned ruleset, and outputs that are not complete code are classified by GPT-4o and counted as insecure (p. 4). The denominator is all outputs. The paper gives the first-pass insecurity rate as 29% in one place and "roughly 30%" in another (p. 6, p. 8).

SecureForge (Liu et al. 2026) reports that models asked to write secure production code, with the CWEs under test named, produced weaknesses detectable by Semgrep on average **23%** of the time on benign prompts, and that **12.7%** of outputs were both vulnerable and passing the generated unit tests (p. 1, p. 2). For four GPT models under a security-aware prompt, the share vulnerable among test-passing outputs ran from 14.7% (GPT 5.3 Codex) to 22.1% (GPT 5.4 Nano) (p. 14). These rates are measured under security-aware prompting, not a neutral prompt (p. 2). Vulnerability means any Semgrep Community Python rule firing, and the unit tests are LLM-generated and not claimed to be exhaustive (p. 6). The results cover single-turn Python generation, which the authors describe as a lower bound on failure rates (pp. 9–10). The abstract cites 250 benign prompts, while the setup describes 500 prompts of which 250 are held out for evaluation (p. 1, p. 6).

## Why they differ

The sources count different things. Pearce counts valid Copilot completions without checking functional correctness. It tests one targeted CWE per scenario with CodeQL or manual marking and calls its classification conservative (p. 4, p. 5). BaxBench runs exploits against functionally correct backends and treats sec_pass as an upper bound (p. 24). Zhao runs security tests taken from fix commits against functionally correct repository-level patches (p. 6). The units of generation range from completion snippets (p. 4) to whole backends (p. 3) to multi-file repository patches (p. 5). CWE coverage ranges from 18 CWEs in Pearce's weakness experiment (p. 5) to 13 in BaxBench (p. 5) and 79 in SUSVIBES (p. 5). The periods also differ: the 2021 Copilot technical preview (p. 3), the LLMs BaxBench evaluated (p. 5), and the 2026 coding agents of SUSVIBES (p. 6).
