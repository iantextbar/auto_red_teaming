## Positions

### Debate: What share of the functional code produced by AI generators is vulnerable?

Debate note: [[share-functional-ai-code-vulnerable]]. **Ten** sources take a stance, six on the side that most correct output is insecure and four on the side that a substantial minority is. Each defines "vulnerable" in its own way and divides by its own denominator: valid completions or snippets, functionally correct backends, repository patches or scripts, outputs that pass generated tests, or all generated output. Several of the figures are therefore over all generated code, not over functional code only. What is contested is how large the vulnerable share is under each source's measure, and whether it is the majority of output.

The position that **most correct output is insecure** is held by the SUSVIBES paper (Zhao et al., 2026), the Broken by Default study (Blain and Noiseux, 2026), the metamorphic-testing study of Peng, Wang and Zhu (2026), the poster by Ma et al. (2026) and Kahn and Hastings (2026), and qualified by BaxBench (Vero et al., 2025). SUSVIBES builds 186 repository-level feature-request tasks from 100 Python repositories, covering 79 CWE types (p. 5). Its security tests come from the human-written tests added by real vulnerability-fix commits, and a solution counts as secure only when it is also functionally correct (p. 6). With SWE-Agent and Claude 4 Sonnet, 57.0% of tasks are solved functionally and 11.8% also securely, from which the paper reports that **79.3%** of the functionally correct solutions are insecure (p. 6, p. 7). Average SecPass across the 12 agent-model settings is around 10% (p. 6). The authors put it as a vibe-coding user who accepts a solution once it passes functional tests leaving security vulnerabilities in the repository "around 80% of the time" (p. 6). They state that test outcomes are a proxy for security and that CWE annotations and tests may be insufficient (p. 24).

BaxBench qualifies the same position. It runs 11 LLMs on 392 backend-generation tasks and executes expert-written exploits against the running applications (p. 3, p. 4, p. 5). It reports that, on average, exploits succeed against **around half** of the correct programs generated by each model (p. 1, p. 5), and that the best correct-and-secure rate is about 37% against a best pass@1 of 62% (p. 2, p. 5). On less popular or multi-file frameworks such as Rust-Actix and PHP-Lumen, the paper finds that correct solutions are more often vulnerable (p. 6). It treats its measured sec_pass as a strict upper bound on true security, since exploits may miss vulnerabilities (p. 24).

The Broken by Default study reports a majority figure over all generated artifacts. Across seven production LLMs and 500 security-sensitive prompts in five CWE categories, **55.8%** of 3,500 artifacts contained at least one vulnerability identified by the authors' COBALT engine, with 1,055 findings proven by Z3 satisfiability witnesses (p. 1, p. 3). Per-model rates run from 48.4% (Gemini 2.5 Flash) to 62.4% (GPT-4o) (p. 3), and category rates from 25% for cryptography to 87% for integer arithmetic (p. 4). The denominator is all artifacts, generated at temperature 0 (p. 2, p. 3). The headline rate includes pattern-match findings without Z3 confirmation, the paper reports no false-positive rate for COBALT (p. 3, p. 4), and the authors are COBALT's developers (p. 1).

Peng, Wang and Zhu report that 2,545 of 3,700 snippets (**68.8%**) violate at least one of nine security-oriented metamorphic relations (p. 3). The snippets come from five open models of 4.5B to 30B on 148 LLMSecEval prompts, five runs each, and the denominator is all generated snippets (p. 3). Violations are judged by an LLM (Claude Sonnet 4.6) that reasons about the code statically without executing it (p. 2), and the paper gives no measured precision or recall for the judge (p. 5). Model rates range from 65.1% to 73.8% (p. 3).

The poster by Ma et al. reports baseline vulnerability rates above 56% for all eight LLMs it evaluates, on 2,700 prompt pairs in nine languages built around three prompt-side risk scenarios (p. 3, p. 4, p. 5). The rate is 66% under ambiguous requirements and 52% under a security–functionality conflict (p. 5). The denominator is the sampled outputs, each labelled by static analysis (Clang Static Analyzer, CodeQL) and expert review assisted by GPT-5, with the final label set by expert review (p. 4). The prompts are designed to resemble vague, incomplete or security-conflicting developer requests, rather than prompts that state security requirements (p. 1).

Kahn and Hastings report that every one of nine Python automation scripts, from ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot and Google Gemini on three tasks, contained exploitable vulnerabilities, and that all nine executed and met their functional requirements (p. 7). The denominator is these nine functional scripts, one generation per model and task (p. 3). The findings come from a single Claude Code review capped at five findings per file, with no manual confirmation and no exploitation, and the authors state that the cap may have hidden further vulnerabilities (p. 3, p. 8).

The position that **a substantial minority is insecure** is held by Pearce et al. (2021), Liu et al. (2024), Datta, Aljohani and Do (2025) and SecureForge (Liu et al., 2026). The Copilot study by Pearce et al. reports that approximately 40% of the programs generated were vulnerable, across 89 scenarios and 1,689 programs (p. 1). Overall, 39.33% of top-ranked options and 40.73% of all options were vulnerable (p. 13). The denominator is valid programs, meaning syntactically compliant, compilable and unique, and functional correctness is not assessed (p. 4, p. 5). An option counts as vulnerable only if it definitively contains the one targeted weakness, a classification the authors call conservative (p. 5). The share varies widely by weakness, from 60% of options for CWE-22 to 19% for CWE-79 (p. 9). The authors acknowledge that CodeQL may have marked some safe code vulnerable, and that sample sizes may not support statistical conclusions (p. 13).

Liu et al. (2024) give ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) the 54 CWE scenarios of Pearce et al. and report that 994 of 2,983 valid snippets (**33.32%**) were vulnerable, 51.64% in C against 17.08% in Python3 (p. 22, p. 26). Detection is by CodeQL, with manual checking by the authors for some scenarios (p. 22), and the denominator is valid snippets (p. 26). The authors note that CodeQL may report secure code as vulnerable (p. 32). The text gives 60 generations per scenario, while the valid counts in its table imply smaller denominators in several rows (p. 22, p. 23).

Datta, Aljohani and Do report that roughly 25–33% of the programs generated by five open code models on Instruct Prime (1,404 prompts, eight languages, 50 CWE types) are insecure at a zero-shot baseline (p. 1, p. 3, p. 6), with per-model secure rates from 68.30% (OpenCoder) to 72.86% (QwenCoder) (p. 6). Insecurity is flagged by the Insecure Code Detector (ICD), a static CWE-aligned ruleset, and outputs that are not complete code are classified by GPT-4o and counted as insecure (p. 4). The denominator is all outputs. The paper gives the first-pass insecurity rate as 29% in one place and "roughly 30%" in another (p. 6, p. 8).

SecureForge reports that models asked to write secure production code, with the CWEs under test named, produced weaknesses detectable by Semgrep on average **23%** of the time on benign prompts, and that 12.7% of outputs were both vulnerable and passing the generated unit tests (p. 1, p. 2). For four GPT models under a security-aware prompt, the share vulnerable among test-passing outputs ran from 14.7% (GPT 5.3 Codex) to 22.1% (GPT 5.4 Nano) (p. 14). These rates are measured under security-aware prompting, not a neutral prompt (p. 2). Vulnerability means any Semgrep Community Python rule firing, and the unit tests are LLM-generated and not claimed to be exhaustive (p. 6). The results cover single-turn Python generation, which the authors describe as a lower bound on failure rates (pp. 9–10). The abstract cites 250 benign prompts, while the setup describes 500 prompts of which 250 are held out for evaluation (p. 1, p. 6).

Era enters this debate through what was generated and counted: completion snippets from the August 2021 Copilot technical preview (Pearce, p. 3, p. 4), whole backends from LLMs (BaxBench, p. 3, p. 5), and multi-file repository patches from 2026 coding agents (SUSVIBES, p. 5, p. 6). CWE coverage also runs from 18 CWEs in Pearce's weakness experiment (p. 5) to 13 in BaxBench (p. 5) and 79 in SUSVIBES (p. 5).

The newer sources in this debate also use different detectors, each with limits the source states: a formal engine built by the authors (Blain, p. 1, p. 3), an LLM judge without a reported precision or recall (Peng, p. 2, p. 5), static analysis with final expert labels (Ma, p. 4), one capped LLM review without confirmation (Kahn, p. 3, p. 8), CodeQL with some manual checking (Liu, p. 22), a static ruleset (Datta, p. 4), and Semgrep rules with generated unit tests (SecureForge, p. 6).

### Debate: Can what goes into the prompt, or the code around it, steer an AI code generator toward secure output?

Debate note: [[prompt-context-steer-secure-code]]. **Ten** sources take a stance, six on the side that prompt and context steer security and four on the side that security hints give marginal gains, and they do not vary the same thing. One alters comments and surrounding code, one observes how users prompt, several add generic, CWE-specific or optimised security instructions to a task, one pairs risk-inducing prompts with security-aware counterparts, one also adds retrieval, and one correlates the wording of existing benchmark prompts with output safety without intervening. "Steer" therefore covers several kinds of intervention.

The position that **prompt and context steer security** is supported by Pearce et al., Perry et al., Ma et al. and SecureForge, and qualified by BaxBench and CWEval. Pearce's diversity-of-prompt experiment mutates one SQL-injection scenario (CWE-89) in 16 ways on the 2021 Copilot preview (p. 9, p. 10). The authors report that most mutations moved results little, but that changes with no semantic meaning, such as rewording a comment, could change whether the top suggestion was vulnerable (p. 11). The largest effect came from code already in the file: adding a non-vulnerable SQL function gave 0 of 18 valid options vulnerable, and adding a vulnerable one gave **17 of 18** (p. 10). The authors hypothesise that existing vulnerable or non-vulnerable SQL in the codebase is the strongest predictor of whether Copilot generates injectable SQL (p. 11). They also report that "best practice" context, such as credentials loaded from environment variables, seems to pull Copilot toward better code (p. 9, p. 13).

Perry et al. (2023) reach a related position from a user study of 47 participants, 33 of whom used an assistant built on codex-davinci-002 (p. 3, p. 10). In the assistant group's prompt log, the paper reports that participants who specified task instructions, function declarations and helper functions generated more secure code (p. 1), and that feeding the assistant's previous output back as the next prompt can magnify or replicate security problems (p. 1, p. 10). Participants who raised the temperature, rephrased and added context tended to end with more secure code, a link the authors treat as possible rather than established (p. 11). These are associations among assistant users: random assignment was to the assistant and control groups, not to prompting strategies (p. 3).

BaxBench qualifies the position. It runs its 11 LLMs under three prompt conditions: no security instruction, a generic reminder to follow security best practices, and an oracle reminder listing the scenario's CWEs (p. 5, p. 19). It finds that generic reminders improve the three reasoning models considerably and give the non-reasoning models no significant improvement (p. 6). The oracle reminder gives the highest sec_pass@1 for every model, but the number of passing programs generally falls; these prompt results are reported as charts, without numbers in the main text (p. 6). With an agent scaffold (OpenHands with Claude 3.5 Sonnet, 351 tasks), there is no security gain under the normal prompt, and sec_pass@1 rises from 35.4 to 39.0 under the oracle prompt (p. 6). The paper reads these results as models failing on security mainly because they do not attend to it unless told to (p. 7, p. 9).

The poster by Ma et al. tests paired prompts: each risk-inducing prompt has a security-aware counterpart that keeps the functional task fixed, so that only the security information varies, and the transformation depends on the risk scenario (p. 3). Across eight LLMs and 2,700 test cases, security-aware prompting reduced vulnerability for every model, by 37% (Qwen3-4B) up to **45%** (Claude-Sonnet-4) (p. 6). The paper does not state whether these reductions are relative or absolute (pp. 5–6). Under the security–functionality conflict the prompting was least robust: regressions reached 19% and net gain fell to 0.12 (p. 6). The authors summarise that security-aware prompting "can effectively improve code security overall, but may introduce regressions when functional requirements conflict with secure coding practices" (p. 2).

SecureForge holds that an optimised system prompt can steer security where plain security requests leave double-digit rates. It builds a corpus of benign prompts that elicited Semgrep findings, and uses the GEPA optimiser to search for the single system prompt that minimises aggregate failure probability across CWEs (p. 5, pp. 6–7). The paper reports that the optimised prompt reduced weakness rates by up to **48%** with test passage essentially unchanged (p. 1, p. 8); the text does not tie the "up to 48%" to a named model or table value. For four GPT models, the share vulnerable among test-passing outputs fell from 14.7–22.1% under a security-aware prompt to 9.0–15.6% (p. 14). The prompt, optimised on synthetic CWE scenarios, improved the joint security-and-test rate by up to 13.5% on 203 real SWE-chat tasks (p. 3, p. 8). The same paper reports that security-aware and CWE-aware prompting alone left vulnerability rates above 13% among test-passing outputs (p. 7, p. 9), and states that a prompt optimised for one model may not secure another (p. 9).

CWEval (Peng et al., 2025) qualifies the position. It adds the single sentence "Your code should be secure and should NOT contain any vulnerability" to the prompt, with no CWE-specific hints (p. 7), and reports that this raises func-sec@k for almost all models, with a possible slight decrease in func@k (p. 6, p. 7). The largest gain is for Claude 3.5 Haiku, up to 9.8% on func-sec@10 (56.48 to 66.28), while Gemini 1.5 Flash moves from 47.81 to 48.97 (p. 7). The func@1 of Llama 3.1 70B falls from 71.84 to 65.79 with the instruction (p. 7). The authors note that the experiment tries only the simplest instruction (p. 7).

The position that **security hints give marginal gains** is held by the SUSVIBES paper, the Broken by Default study, RealSec-bench (Wang et al., 2026) and the metamorphic-testing study of Peng, Wang and Zhu. The SUSVIBES mitigation experiment uses one setting, SWE-Agent with Claude 4 Sonnet, on 186 tasks. Every run receives a generic security reminder, and two CWE-based strategies are compared with that baseline: self-selection, in which the agent picks relevant CWEs from the full list before coding, and an oracle condition giving the true CWE (p. 8). SecPass rises from 11.8% to **14.5%** with self-selection and to 15.1% with the oracle, while FuncPass falls from 57.0% to 50.0% and to 55.4% (p. 8). The authors hypothesise two opposing trends, better awareness of risk and an over-focus on security that drops functional requirements (p. 8). They report that self-selection is about as secure as the oracle hint and suggest this may come from a better understanding of the task context (p. 8, p. 9). They state that the mitigation prompts were kept simple on purpose and that prompt engineering for security was not optimised (p. 19).

The Broken by Default study reports that a security-explicit system prompt lowered the mean vulnerability rate on its 50-prompt v1 corpus from 64.8% to 60.8%, a drop of **4 points**, with memory-allocation vulnerabilities essentially unchanged (p. 4). By model, the rate fell for Mistral Large, GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, stayed at 60% for Gemini 2.5 Flash, and rose from 68% to 70% for Llama 3.3 70B (p. 5). The authors write that "A mean improvement of 4 percentage points under an explicit security prompt is not a safety margin—it is noise" (p. 6), and read the result as security instructions not overriding low-level patterns learned from training data (p. 4, p. 6). They acknowledge that the ablation ran on the 50-prompt corpus only and that the Llama increase may be within noise (p. 7). Vulnerabilities are identified by the authors' own COBALT engine (p. 1, p. 3).

RealSec-bench tests a five-directive security guideline derived from OWASP and three retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) variants on 105 repository-level Java tasks (p. 4, p. 5, p. 12). The guideline moved average SecurePass@1 from 5.14% to **5.71%** and average Pass@1 from 12.95% to 12.38% (p. 16). It helped Deepseek-V3 and GPT-4.1 and hurt Claude-3.7-Sonnet, whose Pass@1 fell from 16.2% to 9.5% (p. 7, p. 14), and the authors conclude that prompt-based security engineering is not universally effective (p. 7). RAG raised average Pass@1 to as much as 19.05% with BM25, while average SecurePass@1 ranged from 4.76% to 6.28% across the three retrievers (p. 16), and the paper states that RAG "offers no substantial improvement in security" (p. 7). Security is scored by CodeQL with multi-LLM adjudication of its alerts (p. 5). The appendix text gives per-model guideline figures that do not match the paper's table (p. 14, p. 16).

Peng, Wang and Zhu do not intervene on prompts. Across 148 LLMSecEval prompts, they correlate prompt features with the number of distinct metamorphic relations violated in the generated code (p. 2, p. 4), and find that security-aware keywords such as "secure", "sanitize" or "validate" show no relationship with output safety (r = -0.04), which they word as "explicitly requesting secure code does not improve output safety" (p. 4). The evidence is a correlation across existing benchmark prompts, scored by an LLM judge (p. 2, p. 4).

Era enters here through what each source could vary: the file context of a 2021 completion tool (Pearce, p. 3), user prompts to codex-davinci-002 (Perry, p. 10), and instructions given to LLMs and agents in 2025–2026 (BaxBench, p. 5, p. 6; SUSVIBES, p. 8). The measures differ too: one targeted CWE without a functional check (Pearce, p. 4, p. 5), exploits against correct backends (BaxBench, p. 24), and security tests from fix commits (SUSVIBES, p. 6).

The newer sources in this debate score security in further ways: static analysis with final expert labels (Ma, p. 4), Semgrep rules with generated unit tests (SecureForge, p. 6), dynamic oracles (CWEval, p. 5), the authors' COBALT engine (Blain, p. 1, p. 3), CodeQL with multi-LLM adjudication (RealSec, p. 5) and an LLM judge (Peng, p. 2). Their interventions also run on different material: single-turn Python generation (SecureForge, pp. 9–10), a 50-prompt corpus (Blain, p. 7), and repository-level Java tasks (RealSec, p. 4, p. 5, p. 12).

### Debate: Do reasoning capabilities improve LLM performance on security-relevant coding tasks?

Debate note: [[reasoning-improve-security-coding-tasks]]. Four sources take a stance, on four tasks: secure backend generation, secure repository-level feature implementation, reproduction of known vulnerabilities by agents, and package-name hallucination. Each makes "reasoning" operational in its own way: reasoning against non-reasoning model families (BaxBench), thinking toggled within the same models (CyberGym), two models differing in both thinking mode and post-training (Churilov), and two generations of one family (SUSVIBES). All four concern frontier models of 2024–2026.

The position that **reasoning improves security performance** is qualified by three sources; in the debate note none supports it without qualification. BaxBench treats o1, o3-mini and DeepSeek-R1 as reasoning models among its 11 LLMs (p. 5). It finds that generic security reminders improve these three considerably while the non-reasoning models show no significant improvement (p. 6), and concludes that "test-time compute in the form of strong reasoning capabilities is highly beneficial for anticipating the often complex security vulnerabilities" (p. 6). The gain it reports is tied to a reminder in the prompt. Without one, o1, the model best at correctness, is not the best at security: three models exceed it in sec_pass@1, among them the non-reasoning Claude 3.5 Sonnet (p. 6).

CyberGym (Wang et al., 2026) qualifies the position on an offensive task, in which agents must write a proof-of-concept input that reproduces a known memory-safety vulnerability in C/C++ projects (p. 4). On a 300-instance subset, turning on thinking raised GPT-5 from **7.7% to 22.0%** (p. 7). The other three models gained modestly: Qwen3-235B-A22B from 2.7% to 4.7%, Claude-3.7-Sonnet from 14.3% to 17.3%, and Claude-Sonnet-4 from 17.7% to 19.3% (p. 7).

Churilov's 2026 replication of Spracklen et al. qualifies the position for package hallucination, which the paper treats as a supply-chain risk because invented names can be registered by an attacker (p. 1). Claude Haiku 4.5 hallucinates package names significantly less than Claude Sonnet 4.6, at 4.62% against 5.41% (p. 6). The paper offers thinking mode and post-training as explanations, but Haiku was queried with thinking enabled and Sonnet without, no ablation was run, and the paper states it cannot disentangle the two (p. 6).

The position that **reasoning is insufficient for security** is held by the SUSVIBES paper. On SWE-Agent, Gemini 3 Pro improves FuncPass by more than 2x over Gemini 2.5 Pro, but SecPass⊥FuncPass, the secure share on tasks both settings solve correctly, rises only 1.3x (p. 6, p. 7). The authors take this to show that "although current reasoning-oriented post-training can significantly improve functional correctness, it remains insufficient for security" (p. 7). The comparison is between two generations of one model family, on 186 repository-level Python tasks with single-attempt pass@1 (p. 5, p. 6).

The sources also measure different things: exploit or test outcomes on generated code (BaxBench, p. 24; SUSVIBES, p. 6), offensive success at reproducing a vulnerability (CyberGym, p. 4), and the rate of hallucinated package references (Churilov, p. 4).

### Debate: Can static analysis tools reliably detect the vulnerabilities in AI-generated code?

Debate note: [[static-analysis-detect-ai-code-vulnerabilities]]. **Eight** sources take a stance, six on the side that static analysis misses much of it (four supporting, two qualifying) and two on the side that it is a usable safeguard (one supporting, one qualifying). The tools in question are CodeQL, Semgrep, Bandit, Cppcheck, Clang Static Analyzer, FlawFinder, Snyk Code and SonarQube. Each source judges them against its own reference, so "reliably" covers different measures: the share of vulnerabilities found by another method that the tools also flag, the stability of tool verdicts across equivalent code, and the precision of the alerts the tools raise.

The position that **static analysis misses much of it** is supported by the Broken by Default study, Peng, Wang and Zhu, CWEval and BaxBench, and qualified by Chong et al. (2024) and Pearce et al. On its 50-prompt v1 corpus of 250 artifacts, the Broken by Default study reports that six tools together flagged 19 artifacts (7.6%) and 2 of the 90 findings proven by Z3 satisfiability witnesses, missing **97.8%** of them (p. 5, p. 6). CodeQL security-extended flagged none of the 68 C and 22 Python Z3-proven artifacts, and Cppcheck and Clang SA flagged none of 87 C artifacts (p. 5, p. 6). The authors call this "a structural gap, not a configuration issue", since detecting overflow in allocation arithmetic needs arithmetic reasoning under modular semantics, which pattern and taint-based analysis lack (p. 5, p. 6). The reference is the authors' own COBALT engine (p. 1, p. 3); the paper reports no false-positive rate for it and validates 7 findings at runtime, 6 of which produced faults (p. 3, p. 4). The text also refers to "80 confirmed C vulnerabilities" for an experiment run on 87 C artifacts (p. 5).

Peng, Wang and Zhu report that on 3,700 snippets, their LLM judge applying nine metamorphic relations finds 68.8% violating at least one, while CodeQL, Bandit, Semgrep and Flawfinder together flag **34.2%** (p. 3). Separately, CodeQL flags 3 snippets (0.1%), Bandit 45, Semgrep 90 and Flawfinder 1,130 (p. 4). No SAST tool detects any authentication-bypass violation, and the tools catch 44 of 651 hard-coded-credential violations (p. 3). The judge reasons about code statically without executing it (p. 2), and the paper reports a 36-snippet pilot review with "high accuracy" but no figure, so the comparison rests on a judge with no measured precision or recall (p. 5).

CWEval argues that static-analysis-based security evaluation is unstable. It reports that only 562 of the 1,916 vulnerable samples in CyberSecEval are still flagged by that benchmark's associated static analyser, and shows CodeQL's incomplete-URL-sanitisation rule giving false negatives and a false positive on semantically similar redirect implementations (p. 2). Its response is to measure security with dynamic, outcome-driven oracles and to "not rely on any static analyzer" (p. 5). Its evidence on static analysis is a reproduction count on one benchmark's samples and a worked example on one rule (p. 2).

BaxBench compares Snyk Code with its executed exploits on the 237 correct programs generated by o1. Snyk missed 64 vulnerable programs that the exploits compromised; of 25 programs Snyk flagged that the exploits did not trigger, 16 (6.75%) were false positives, 2 concerned rate limits, and 7 (2.95%) were correct additional flags (p. 8). The paper states that these results "signify the unsuitability of SAST tools for benchmarking" (p. 8). Its own reference is bounded, since exploits may miss vulnerabilities (p. 24), and it used Snyk Code in developing its exploits (p. 4).

Chong et al. qualify the position from a different reference. GPT-4o's C solutions to 202 LeetCode problems were run on the online judge and compiled with AddressSanitizer, and the paper reports that the Clang static analyzer missed 8 of the 12 runtime errors (p. 7). The authors conclude that static analysis alone is insufficient (p. 11). The errors are runtime failures on LeetCode tasks, such as buffer overflow and signed integer overflow, rather than findings identified as vulnerabilities (p. 7, p. 8), and the same paper uses Clang as its main security scorer, reporting a negligibly low false-positive rate in its manual validation (p. 6). Pearce et al. qualify it from inside a study that relies on CodeQL as its main detector (p. 4, p. 5): among their limitations, they acknowledge that CodeQL cannot reason about memory buffer sizes, that some CWEs need information beyond the file, and that it may have marked some safe code vulnerable (p. 13).

The position that **static analysis is a usable safeguard** is supported by Sabra, Schmitt and Tyler (2025) and qualified by RealSec-bench. The Sonar study scans Java code from five LLMs on 4,442 tasks with SonarQube's default ruleset of about 550 rules (p. 5), reports bugs, vulnerabilities and code smells from every model (p. 9), and concludes that prompt engineering is not a sufficient safeguard on its own and that static analysis and expert human review are essential (p. 16). It adds that static analysis should be complemented by Software Composition Analysis for deprecated APIs and vulnerable dependencies (p. 12, p. 21). The authors are affiliated with Sonar, the company behind SonarQube (p. 1); SonarQube is the study's only detector (p. 5), and the paper reports no measure of what the tool misses or of its false positives.

RealSec-bench uses CodeQL as its security oracle but counts an alert only if a panel of voter LLMs and a final-judge LLM do not reclassify it as a false positive (p. 5). On 89 manually reviewed alerts, CodeQL alone has **44.9%** precision, and voters plus judge raise precision to 81.7% at 98.0% recall, figures computed over alerts that CodeQL raised (p. 6). The authors list dependence on SAST tools and their false positives as a threat to internal validity, and state that the adjudication remains limited by LLM accuracy and falls short of manual expert annotation (p. 9).

The sources judge static analysis against different references: Z3 witnesses from an engine the authors develop (Blain, p. 1, p. 3), an LLM judge without reported precision or recall (Peng, p. 5), a reproduction count on benchmark samples (CWEval, p. 2), executed exploits treated as an upper bound on security (BaxBench, p. 24), runtime errors rather than identified vulnerabilities (Chong, p. 7), and a study's own stated limitations (Pearce, p. 13). On the other side, Sabra reports what SonarQube finds without a measure of what it misses, and is published by the company that sells the tool (p. 1, p. 5), while RealSec measures precision over the alerts CodeQL raised (p. 6). The code analysed ranges from C and Python artifacts dominated by allocation and integer-arithmetic findings (Blain, p. 5, p. 6) to backend applications (BaxBench, p. 3), Java benchmark tasks (Sabra, p. 5) and functions in real Java repositories (RealSec, p. 1).

### Debate: Is the security of AI-generated code driven more by the task or prompt than by which model writes it?

Debate note: [[task-prompt-model-drive-code-security]]. **Five** sources take a stance, four on the side that task and prompt drive vulnerability (two supporting, two qualifying) and one on the side that model choice matters. They isolate different factors: some hold the prompt fixed and compare models, one varies the prompt within pairs, and one varies model size within families. All concern code generated from benchmark or task prompts in 2025–2026.

The position that **task and prompt drive vulnerability** is supported by Peng, Wang and Zhu and by Kahn and Hastings, and qualified by Sabra et al. and Ma et al. On 3,700 snippets from five open models on 148 LLMSecEval prompts, Peng, Wang and Zhu report that all five models agree on violation status for **65.5%** of prompts, with a mean agreement score of 0.846; 94 prompts (63.5%) were violated by all models and 3 (2.0%) were consistently secure (p. 5). They read this as "the prompt itself, not the model architecture, is the primary driver of insecure code" (p. 5). Prompt keywords correlate with the number of violations, database keywords at r = 0.52 and authentication keywords at r = 0.43 (p. 4). The paper also reports model violation rates from 65.1% to 73.8%, notes that the largest model is not the safest (p. 3), and reports that on the 51 prompts where models disagree, DeepSeek-Coder violates 76.5% and Qwen3-Coder 49.0% (p. 5). Violations are judged by an LLM (p. 2), and the authors state that results are specific to LLMSecEval prompts and five open models of 4.5B to 30B (p. 5).

Kahn and Hastings give three automation tasks once each, with identical prompts, to ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot and Google Gemini, giving nine Python scripts (p. 3). Nine of the 17 deduplicated vulnerability classes appeared in code from all three models and 14 in at least two, and weighted CVSS totals differed by less than 10% across models (p. 4). The same classes recur in the same task contexts, such as template injection in each email sender and SSRF in each scraper (p. 5). The authors conclude that the risk "is not tied to any particular model but rather to the task category" (p. 1). Their evidence is one generation per model and task, reviewed by Claude Code with a cap of five findings per file, with no manual confirmation and no exploitation (p. 3, p. 8).

Sabra et al. qualify the position. With identical prompts across five LLMs on 4,442 Java tasks (p. 5), each model produced a comparable mix of about 90–93% code smells, 5–8% bugs and around 2% vulnerabilities (p. 9), and hard-coded credentials appeared in code from all five (p. 17). The paper reads the similarity as a systemic pattern of current code generation rather than a model-specific flaw (p. 9), and attributes it to training data, "the most plausible origin" (p. 16), not to the prompt. It also reports model differences in issue density, from 19.48 to 32.45 issues per KLOC (p. 8), and in vulnerabilities per KLOC, from 0.38 to 0.62 (p. 9). Its explanations are framed as possible rather than established (pp. 11–15).

Ma et al. also qualify it. Across eight LLMs on 2,700 prompt pairs in three risk scenarios, every model has a baseline vulnerability rate above 56%, and the rate differs by scenario, 66% under ambiguous requirements against 52% under a security–functionality conflict (p. 3, p. 4, p. 5). The authors state that the security of generated code is shaped by prompt-side risk conditions as well as by model capability (p. 2).

The position that **model choice matters** is held by CWEval. On 119 tasks over 31 CWEs in five languages, scored by dynamic functionality and security oracles (p. 3, p. 5, p. 6), it reports that "larger models almost always achieves higher func-sec@k" (p. 6). In the GPT-4o and Gemini families the func@k differences are small, with the smaller model sometimes slightly ahead, while the func-sec@k differences are larger (p. 6). At func-sec@10, GPT-4o scores 65.33 against 60.28 for GPT-4o mini, and Gemini 1.5 Pro 53.08 against 47.81 for Gemini 1.5 Flash (p. 7). Across families, Claude 3.5 Haiku beats Llama 3.1 70B on func-sec@10 despite a lower func@10 (p. 6). The size comparison uses a single sampling temperature, 0.8, because of budget (p. 6).

The sources report different quantities: agreement on violation status (Peng, p. 5), overlap of vulnerability classes and CVSS totals (Kahn, p. 4), proportions of issue types (Sabra, p. 9), vulnerability rates by scenario (Ma, p. 5) and functional-and-secure pass rates (CWEval, p. 6). They also compare different models: open models of 4.5B to 30B (Peng, p. 5), consumer chatbots (Kahn, p. 3), five LLMs on Java tasks (Sabra, p. 5), eight LLMs (Ma, p. 4), and three families at several sizes (CWEval, p. 6). Sabra explains the shared profile by training data rather than by the prompt (p. 16).

### Debate: Does feeding detector feedback back to the model remove the vulnerabilities in its code?

Debate note: [[detector-feedback-repair-generated-code]]. **Three** sources take a stance, one on the side that feedback repair removes most flagged flaws and two on the side that it is partial and can regress (one supporting, one qualifying). All three are single-model repair loops on generated code in 2024–2025, the feedback in each comes from a static detector, and removal is reported in the terms of that detector's findings.

The position that **feedback repair removes most flagged flaws** is held by Liu et al. (2024). They feed CodeQL's messages back to ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo-0301) as the next prompt for at most five rounds (p. 4, p. 10). On the CWE scenarios taken from Pearce et al., the paper reports that this fixed 143 of 160 sampled vulnerable snippets, **89.4%** (p. 25, p. 27). On code generated for LeetCode problems in C, C++ and Java, with 15 snippets sampled per CodeQL query, fixing succeeded for all sampled snippets (p. 22, p. 25, p. 27). Fixing was weak for the Input Validation and Information Leakage groups (p. 25, p. 26). The paper takes these results to suggest that combining ChatGPT with vulnerability detection tools can mitigate vulnerabilities in the generated code (p. 25, p. 27), and acknowledges that CodeQL may report secure code as vulnerable (p. 32). Its non-determinism study finds that temperature 0 made one-round generation consistent across trials but not multi-round fixing (p. 27, p. 29).

The position that **feedback repair is partial and can regress** is supported by Chong et al. and qualified by Datta, Aljohani and Do. Chong et al. run three feedback loops on 60 of GPT-4o's C solutions to LeetCode problems, 30 with Clang-reported issues and 30 without, one loop per issue category, each adding to the original prompt an instruction not to have the named issue (p. 9, p. 10, p. 11). On the files with issues, the total fell from 58 to 44: malloc overflow from 40 to 24 and null dereference from 3 to 2, while array-index-out-of-bounds issues rose from 15 to 18 (p. 10, p. 11). On the files without issues, the regenerated code had **4** malloc overflows where there had been none (p. 10, p. 12). The authors conclude that the LLM "so far is not capable of consistently removing security issues even though the prompt has explicitly asked it to do so" (p. 11). The study uses one model and small independent tasks, which the authors contrast with large software projects (p. 13).

Datta, Aljohani and Do run a three-round reflexion loop with five open code models on the 1,404 prompts of Instruct Prime, in eight languages (p. 3). Each round gives the model the task, a buffer of all prior attempts with the CWEs flagged by ICD, and an instruction to revise minimally while preserving functionality (p. 4). Averaged over models, secure-generation accuracy rises from 70.74% at the zero-shot baseline to **79.43%** after three rounds, by +6.00 pp in round one, +1.66 in round two and +1.03 in round three (p. 7). Every model improves, QwenCoder most (+11.61 pp) and OpenCoder least (+4.21 pp) (p. 7). Regressions, outputs going from secure to insecure, rise to about 1–2% in later rounds (p. 7, p. 8). The authors conclude that "one or two reflexion rounds capture most of the improvement, while additional iterations provide diminishing returns and occasionally undo earlier repairs" (p. 7). The same detector supplies the feedback and scores each output (p. 3, p. 4), and the authors acknowledge that ICD is static and can miss context or give false positives (p. 8).

What is counted differs across the three: sampled vulnerable snippets that were fixed (Liu, p. 25), issue totals including those newly introduced in files that had none (Chong, p. 11, p. 12), and repairs and regressions between rounds as shares of all prompts, scored by the detector that gave the feedback (Datta, p. 3, p. 4). The models are GPT-3.5 (Liu, p. 4), GPT-4o (Chong, p. 13) and five open code models (Datta, p. 3).

### Candidate debate: Does agent scaffolding improve the security of generated code?

Candidate, not a debate: two sources, BaxBench and SUSVIBES, take a stance ([[candidate-debates]]). BaxBench reports that agent scaffolding (OpenHands with Claude 3.5 Sonnet, 351 tasks) gives notable but not drastic correctness gains and no security gain without the oracle prompt (p. 6). SUSVIBES compares scaffolds rather than scaffold against none: on the common functional subset, the secure share is 23.3% for SWE-Agent, 20.1% for OpenHands and 17.4% for Claude Code (p. 6), and the paper reports that different models and frameworks avoid different CWE categories, so their strengths are complementary (p. 6).

### Candidate debate: Do developers write less secure code with an AI assistant than without one?

Candidate, not a debate: one source, Perry et al., takes a stance ([[candidate-debates]]). In its study of 47 participants on security tasks, those with access to an AI assistant wrote significantly less secure code than those without (p. 1). Assistant users were less secure on four of the five analysed tasks, with mixed results on the C task (p. 5, p. 7). The paper also reports that assistant users were more likely to believe they had written secure code (p. 1).

### Candidate debate: Do security rankings of models match their functional rankings?

Candidate, not a debate: two sources take a stance and they do not disagree ([[candidate-debates]]). BaxBench reports that the model best at correctness, o1, is not the best at security, and that three models exceed it in sec_pass@1 (p. 6). SUSVIBES reports that Gemini 2.5 Pro is the most secure model on the common functional subset (30.4%), although Claude 4 Sonnet has the higher FuncPass (p. 6). Both say the rankings differ under their own benchmarks, so the question is not yet contested between them.

### Candidate debate: Do newer model generations make fewer security failures?

Candidate, not a debate: two sources take a stance ([[candidate-debates]]), and era is the object of the question. Churilov reports that the inter-model range of package-hallucination rates across five 2026 frontier models is 4.62%–6.10%, against 5.2%–21.7% in Spracklen's 2024 cohort (p. 1, p. 4). The paper limits this compression to the five sampled frontier models and notes that smaller open-weight models may still show Spracklen-like rates (p. 8). SUSVIBES reports that Gemini 3 Pro improves FuncPass by more than 2x over Gemini 2.5 Pro on SWE-Agent, but SecPass⊥FuncPass only by 1.3x (p. 6, p. 7). The two sources measure different things.

### Candidate debate: Is generated code less secure in less common languages or frameworks?

Candidate, not a debate: two sources take a stance ([[candidate-debates]]). BaxBench reports that models perform worse, and that their correct solutions are more often vulnerable, on less popular or complex frameworks such as Rust-Actix and PHP-Lumen (p. 6). Pearce et al. report that in hardware (Verilog) scenarios 28.28% of 198 valid programs were vulnerable, but that Copilot often failed to produce valid Verilog (p. 11, p. 13); across software languages, 50.29% of options in C were vulnerable against 38.35% in Python (p. 5).

### Candidate debate: Does training-data contamination bias security benchmark results?

Candidate, not a debate: three sources mention the question, but only one tests it ([[candidate-debates]]). CyberGym finds no statistically significant effect of potential data contamination on success rates for the four models it tested (p. 8). The BaxBench authors note that public release makes contamination impossible to rule out (p. 9). Churilov states that the paper did not collect a held-out subset of post-cutoff prompts to test for training-data leakage (p. 9).

### Candidate debate: Why do models emit insecure code?

Candidate, not a debate: two sources offer explanations, and neither tests one ([[candidate-debates]]). Pearce et al. theorise that Copilot's uneven security quality comes from the community-provided code it was trained on, and that outdated practices persist in the training set (p. 13). BaxBench reads its prompt results as models failing mainly by not attending to security unless told to, which it takes as a sign that base models may already hold the knowledge and that post-training should make security an explicit objective (p. 7, p. 9).

## Gaps

- The corpus has 18 sources (2021–2026), six debates and seven candidate debates; no question hub exists yet, so this document serves none.
- One source, Kharma et al. (2026), takes no stance in any debate or candidate; its key claims compare security across C, C++, Java and Python (p. 1, p. 12).
- [[candidate-debates]] does not list several newer sources that report on its questions, so the candidate sections above give its current state: Liu et al. 2024 on C against Python (p. 22, p. 26) and Kharma (p. 1) on languages; Sabra (p. 16) and Blain (p. 4, p. 6) on training data as an origin of insecure output; CWEval on func-sec@10 and func@10 rankings diverging (p. 6).
- The `## Question` and `## Why they differ` sections of [[share-functional-ai-code-vulnerable]] and [[prompt-context-steer-secure-code]] still describe only their original three and four sources; the method and denominator of the newer sources are given only in their position paragraphs.
- Detection is now a debate, but one about static tools judged against references each source supplies. No source measures an LLM-based detector's recall on generated code against an independent reference: Peng, Wang and Zhu give no figure for their judge (p. 5), Kahn's Claude Code review is unconfirmed (p. 3, p. 8), and RealSec's adjudication figures are computed over CodeQL's own alerts (p. 6). Blain reports no false-positive rate for COBALT (p. 3, p. 4).
- Repair is now a debate, but only for single-model loops in 2024–2025 fed by a static detector (Liu p. 4, p. 10; Chong p. 9; Datta p. 4); no source in it repairs from exploit or test feedback or with coding agents.
- No debate covers automated red-teaming of AI-generated code. SecureForge searches automatically for benign prompts that elicit Semgrep findings from a target model (p. 4, pp. 4–5), BaxBench runs fixed expert-written exploits (p. 3, p. 4, p. 5), and CyberGym evaluates agents reproducing known vulnerabilities in real-world C/C++ projects (p. 3, p. 4), not in generated code.
- No source checks generated code for compliance with a secure-coding standard; CWE and OWASP serve as taxonomies for scenarios, exploits and findings (Pearce p. 1, p. 2; BaxBench p. 5, p. 14; Kahn p. 7), and RealSec derives a prompt guideline from OWASP (p. 4, p. 5, p. 12).
- Developer behaviour still rests on one user study (Perry, 2023, codex-davinci-002); Kahn and Hastings study scripts of the kind non-expert users deploy but generate them with identical prompts and no participants (p. 1, p. 3), and the corpus has no field data on AI-generated code in production.
- Two sources are classed `vendor`: Sabra et al., whose authors are affiliated with Sonar, the company behind the tool they use (p. 1), and Blain and Noiseux, who develop the COBALT engine they use (p. 1). BaxBench and SUSVIBES are `undetermined`; the rest are `independent`.
